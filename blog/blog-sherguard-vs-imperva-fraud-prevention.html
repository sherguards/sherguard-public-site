SherGuard vs Imperva: Which Security Platform Is Right for Your Business?

Compare two different approaches to protecting online businesses. Explore Imperva's application security, bot mitigation, and API protection alongside SherGuard's connected intelligence for fake signups, device risk, automated abuse, API misuse, and payment fraud.

Introduction: Choosing the Right Protection for Modern Online Businesses

Online businesses face a growing range of threats that do not always look like traditional cyberattacks. A website may remain available while automated accounts consume promotional credits. A marketplace may continue processing orders while fake buyers and sellers manipulate its ecosystem. A SaaS platform may experience a sudden increase in registrations that appear legitimate but are generated by scripts. An API may receive requests that follow the correct technical format but are being used to extract data, abuse business logic, or exhaust resources.

These problems create an important distinction between protecting the infrastructure that runs a business and understanding whether the activity interacting with that business is trustworthy. Both areas matter. A company may need protection against malicious requests, application vulnerabilities, automated traffic, credential attacks, and API exploitation. It may also need to identify suspicious identities, risky devices, fake registrations, abusive customer behavior, and fraudulent payment attempts.

This is why businesses evaluating security platforms may compare SherGuard vs Imperva. Both address important risks in the online environment, but their product positioning and areas of emphasis are different.

Imperva offers an established application and data security portfolio. Its published capabilities include web application security, Advanced Bot Protection, API Security, Account Takeover Protection, and DDoS protection. These capabilities are relevant to organizations defending websites, applications, APIs, and account access against malicious activity.

SherGuard is positioned as a Trust Intelligence Platform. It connects five risk areas: Email Risk Intelligence for suspicious signup activity, Device Risk Intelligence, Bot Detection Intelligence, API Abuse Intelligence, and Payment Fraud Intelligence. Its purpose is to help businesses evaluate activity across important customer and application workflows through one platform.

This comparison examines where each platform fits, what problems its capabilities address, and what a business should evaluate before choosing. It does not assume that one platform replaces every capability of the other. The right choice depends on the organization's architecture, threat model, existing security controls, technical requirements, and the specific risks it needs to manage.

1. What Is Imperva?

Imperva is a cybersecurity provider with products covering application security, API security, bot protection, account protection, and distributed denial-of-service defense. Its portfolio is designed to help organizations protect applications and data from attacks that target public-facing services and the systems behind them.

For businesses operating websites, APIs, digital services, and customer-facing applications, these security layers can help address threats that range from malicious web requests to automated abuse and account compromise.

Imperva Advanced Bot Protection

Automated traffic is not automatically malicious. Search engine crawlers, monitoring systems, integrations, and legitimate automation are part of normal internet activity. The challenge is distinguishing useful automation from bots performing harmful actions.

Imperva's Advanced Bot Protection is designed to identify and mitigate malicious automated activity. Relevant bot threats include scraping, automated account attacks, credential stuffing, and other forms of abusive automation. These attacks can affect websites, applications, and business operations even when the traffic appears to use ordinary browser or application requests.

Bot protection is particularly important for businesses that depend on public-facing services. An e-commerce website may experience automated inventory abuse. A ticketing service may face automated purchasing attempts. A marketplace may be targeted by scraping or fake account creation. A financial service may encounter automated login attempts against customer accounts.

Imperva's bot protection offering is part of a broader application-security portfolio rather than a standalone answer to every type of customer or transaction fraud.

Imperva API Security

APIs connect applications, mobile clients, partner systems, internal services, and external integrations. They also create potential attack surfaces. An API can be technically available and still expose sensitive data or business operations through insecure authorization, excessive permissions, undocumented endpoints, or weaknesses in application logic.

Imperva API Security focuses on discovering and assessing APIs, identifying security risks, and detecting threats against API environments. Its published capabilities include API discovery, classification, risk assessment, and protection against issues such as Broken Object Level Authorization, commonly known as BOLA.

Imperva's API security approach is therefore relevant to organizations that need visibility into their API inventory and protection against vulnerabilities and attacks involving API traffic and business logic.

Imperva Account Takeover Protection

Its published capabilities include defenses against credential stuffing and brute-force activity, as well as leaked credential detection intended to help organizations identify accounts that may be at risk.

Imperva's broader application security portfolio

Imperva's security portfolio also includes web application security and DDoS protection. These controls address different layers of the attack surface. A web application firewall can help filter malicious requests, while DDoS protection focuses on attacks intended to overwhelm network or application resources.

For example, a disposable email address may be used to create a fake account. The same actor may return from a suspicious device, automate activity through a browser, abuse an API endpoint, and eventually attempt a fraudulent payment. Looking at each event separately can make the overall pattern harder to understand.

SherGuard's approach is to bring five connected intelligence modules into one platform, supported by API integrations, a central dashboard, risk information, and configurable operational decisions.

Device Risk Intelligence evaluates information about the environment from which activity originates. Device context can help businesses identify activity that email verification or password checks alone may not reveal. For example, a large number of accounts may be created using different email addresses but similar device characteristics. A suspicious login may originate from an unfamiliar environment. A scripted session may present browser signals associated with automation.

Device signals should not be treated as proof of wrongdoing by themselves. Legitimate users can share networks, change devices, use privacy tools, or travel. Device risk is most useful when interpreted alongside other signals and business context.

3. Bot Detection Intelligence

SherGuard Bot Detection Intelligence is designed to analyze activity patterns that may indicate automation. Signals can include session timing, unusually high click activity, missing or limited interaction signals, and behavior inconsistent with ordinary user activity.

This is relevant to websites and applications exposed to automated registration, scraping, promotional abuse, fake engagement, and other forms of scripted activity.

Bot detection is a difficult technical problem because malicious automation can imitate legitimate browsers and human interaction. 4. API Abuse Intelligence

API Abuse Intelligence focuses on suspicious patterns involving backend endpoints and API clients. Relevant signals may include burst traffic, repeated requests, unusual endpoint usage, suspicious client characteristics, missing expected headers, and credential-related abuse patterns.

API abuse is not always the same as an API vulnerability. An endpoint may be implemented correctly but still be used in an abusive way. For example, an automated client may repeatedly request public data, attempt to enumerate resources, or send excessive requests that consume service capacity.

SherGuard's API Abuse Intelligence is designed to give businesses additional risk information about suspicious API activity. It should be considered alongside secure API design, authentication, authorization, gateway policies, rate limits, logging, and vulnerability management.

5. Payment Fraud Intelligence

Payment Fraud Intelligence helps businesses evaluate suspicious checkout and payment activity. Relevant indicators may include repeated failed attempts, unusual payment velocity, billing mismatches, proxy or VPN indicators, and other patterns that may warrant additional review.

Payment fraud can cause direct financial losses, chargebacks, operational costs, payment processor disputes, and customer service problems. Card testing attacks can also generate large numbers of low-value attempts that are intended to identify valid payment credentials.

SherGuard's payment risk capability is intended to provide additional intelligence for business decision-making. It does not remove the need for payment processor controls, strong checkout security, fraud rules, transaction monitoring, or appropriate review procedures.

One platform for multiple trust decisions

SherGuard's central proposition is that businesses can connect several risk areas through one platform instead of treating every event as an unrelated problem. Its dashboard and API-based integration model are designed to help teams review risk information and apply their own operational policies.

The platform also includes API key management, organization-level controls, team management, usage monitoring, and AI Agent Permissions for governing configured actions. 4. Why Businesses Need More Than Basic Website Security

A website can have encrypted connections, a functioning firewall, and a secure hosting environment while still experiencing fraud and abuse. This happens because not every threat attempts to exploit a software vulnerability.

Some attacks use legitimate application features in ways the business did not intend. A signup form can be used to create thousands of fake accounts. A login endpoint can be targeted with stolen passwords. An API can be queried repeatedly to collect data. A checkout process can be abused to test payment cards.

These attacks may use valid HTTP requests, ordinary browser behavior, and real application workflows. The request may be technically valid while the business activity behind it is harmful.

Infrastructure attacks and trust abuse are different problems

Infrastructure and application security controls are essential for protecting systems against attacks such as malicious payloads, exploitation attempts, and denial-of-service activity. A web application firewall, DDoS mitigation service, secure hosting architecture, and vulnerability management program all have important roles.

Trust and fraud intelligence addresses another question: should this activity be trusted?

Consider a user who submits a valid registration request using a disposable email address, an unusual device environment, and a browser session with automated interaction patterns. The request may not contain a conventional exploit payload. A firewall focused on malicious request patterns may not be designed to make the complete business-risk decision.

Similarly, a customer may log in with a correct password that was stolen elsewhere. The login request may be syntactically valid, but the context and behavior may indicate account compromise.

This is why modern security programs often combine multiple layers: infrastructure protection, application security, identity controls, device intelligence, bot detection, API security, transaction monitoring, and operational response.

Why disconnected tools can create blind spots

When each risk area is handled by a separate system, teams may see individual alerts without understanding the broader sequence. A signup tool may identify a suspicious email. A bot tool may flag an automated session. An API gateway may record excessive requests. A payment provider may decline a transaction.

If these events are not connected operationally, investigators may need to correlate them manually. This can increase response time and make it harder to recognize repeat abuse.

A connected trust intelligence approach aims to make these signals easier to evaluate together. It does not mean that one platform automatically replaces every specialist security product. It means the business has an additional way to assess risk across different workflows.

5. Real-World Attack Scenarios: Where the Platforms Fit

Scenario 1: Automated fake account creation

A SaaS business offers a free trial to new customers. An attacker builds a script that repeatedly submits the registration form using disposable email addresses and changing account details. The goal is to obtain multiple free trials, consume resources, or exploit referral incentives.

The attack may begin with ordinary registration requests. If the business only checks whether an email address is correctly formatted, many fake accounts may pass.

Useful defenses include email risk analysis, bot detection, device context, registration velocity limits, email verification, and controls that identify repeated patterns. SherGuard's Email Risk Intelligence and Bot Detection Intelligence are directly relevant to these risk areas. Imperva's bot and account protection capabilities may also be relevant where automated account abuse is part of the threat.

The best implementation combines detection with proportionate action. A suspicious registration might receive additional verification rather than an immediate permanent block. This helps reduce the risk of rejecting legitimate users who happen to share an IP address or use privacy tools.

Scenario 2: Credential stuffing and account takeover

Attackers obtain username and password combinations from previous data breaches. They use automated tools to test those credentials against another website, hoping that customers have reused their passwords.

If a credential works, the attacker may access personal information, change account details, misuse stored payment methods, or take control of the account.

Imperva specifically offers Account Takeover Protection for login-related attacks, including credential stuffing and brute-force activity. Its leaked credential detection capability is also relevant to identifying accounts that may be exposed.

SherGuard's device, bot, and API risk signals may contribute to a broader assessment of suspicious access and automated behavior when integrated into the relevant application flows. However, businesses should not assume that these modules are a complete replacement for dedicated account takeover protection, identity security, password security, or multifactor authentication.

Strong defenses include multifactor authentication, breached-password screening, login rate controls, session security, device context, anomaly detection, and clear account recovery procedures.

Scenario 3: API scraping and excessive data requests

An online marketplace exposes APIs used by its website and mobile application. An automated client discovers an endpoint that returns product listings, seller details, or other business data. The attacker sends repeated requests to collect information at scale.

The traffic may use valid API parameters and authenticated sessions. The problem is not necessarily a software vulnerability; it may be excessive or abusive use of a legitimate function.

Imperva API Security is relevant to API discovery, risk assessment, and the protection of APIs against vulnerabilities and threats. Its Advanced Bot Protection can also help address automated activity.

SherGuard API Abuse Intelligence is designed to identify suspicious API usage patterns such as bursts, repeated requests, and endpoint misuse. Bot Detection Intelligence can add context when the activity is automated.

These approaches address related but different questions. API security needs to examine whether the endpoint is exposed or vulnerable. API abuse analysis needs to help determine whether a client is using an available endpoint in a suspicious way. Both may be required.

Scenario 4: Payment card testing

An attacker uses automated checkout attempts to test stolen or generated card details. The attempts may involve small amounts, repeated failures, multiple accounts, or unusual transaction patterns.

The business may experience increased payment processing costs, declines, fraud alerts, chargebacks, and disruption to legitimate checkout activity.

SherGuard includes a dedicated Payment Fraud Intelligence module designed to analyze suspicious payment patterns and risk indicators. Its bot and device signals may also provide additional context when connected to the checkout workflow.

Imperva's bot and application security capabilities can be relevant to automated activity targeting checkout and other application functions. However, businesses evaluating Imperva for payment fraud decisioning should confirm the exact transaction-risk capabilities, payment integrations, and workflows included in the proposed solution.

Payment processors, card network controls, 3-D Secure where appropriate, velocity rules, transaction monitoring, and manual review remain important parts of a complete payment security strategy.

Scenario 5: API authorization and business logic exploitation

A mobile application uses an API endpoint to retrieve customer records. The endpoint accepts a record identifier supplied by the client. If the server does not correctly verify whether the authenticated user is authorized to access that specific record, an attacker may change the identifier and retrieve another customer's information.

This is an example of Broken Object Level Authorization. It is a serious API security issue because the request may be authenticated and correctly formatted while still violating the application's authorization rules.

Imperva API Security specifically describes protection and detection capabilities for API risks such as BOLA. This is an important distinction: identifying suspicious request volume is not the same as proving that an API correctly enforces object-level permissions.

SherGuard API Abuse Intelligence may help identify suspicious API usage patterns, but it should not be represented as a substitute for secure authorization design, code review, API testing, or a dedicated API vulnerability management solution.

6. Detailed Comparison: Bot Protection

Bot protection is one of the clearest areas of overlap between SherGuard and Imperva. Both platforms address automated activity, but businesses should examine the precise detection and mitigation requirements rather than comparing product names alone.

What a business should expect from bot protection

A useful bot defense strategy should distinguish legitimate automation from malicious automation. It should consider the context of a request, the behavior of a session, the endpoint being accessed, the rate of activity, and the likely impact on the business.

Legitimate bots include search engine crawlers, uptime monitors, approved integrations, and partner automation. Malicious bots may perform scraping, credential stuffing, fake account creation, inventory abuse, spam submissions, or automated checkout attempts.

Imperva's approach

Imperva's Advanced Bot Protection is positioned as a dedicated capability for identifying and mitigating malicious automated traffic. This is relevant for organizations that need bot defenses as part of a wider application security environment.

Organizations should evaluate how the product handles their particular traffic patterns, what deployment method is appropriate, how policies are configured, how exceptions are managed, and how bot decisions integrate with their web and API infrastructure.

SherGuard's approach

SherGuard provides a dedicated Bot Detection Intelligence module within a wider trust intelligence platform. It is intended to evaluate behavioral and automation-related signals and make risk information available to the business through its dashboard and APIs.

Its value proposition is especially relevant when bot signals need to be considered alongside email risk, device risk, API abuse, and payment risk. For example, an automated signup attempt may be more concerning when it also uses a disposable email address and a suspicious device environment.

What buyers should test

  • Can the solution distinguish approved crawlers and integrations from harmful automation?
  • Can it detect the bot behaviors that matter to the business?
  • Can policies be adjusted without disrupting legitimate customers?

7. Detailed Comparison: API Security and API Abuse Detection

API protection is another important area where buyers must understand the difference between securing an API and analyzing the behavior of clients using it.

API vulnerabilities versus API abuse

API vulnerabilities arise when an API has weaknesses in its design, implementation, configuration, or authorization controls. Examples include broken object-level authorization, excessive data exposure, weak authentication, insecure function-level authorization, and unsafe configuration.

API abuse can occur even when an endpoint is functioning as designed. An attacker may send excessive requests, scrape data, automate a legitimate workflow, or use an authorized account in ways that violate the business's acceptable-use policies.

These categories overlap, but they are not identical. A business needs to know whether it is looking for vulnerable endpoints, suspicious clients, abnormal usage, or all three.

Imperva API Security

Imperva describes API Security as a platform for API discovery, risk assessment, detection, and mitigation. Its published capabilities include continuous discovery of public, private, and shadow APIs, classification, risk assessment, and detection of API threats such as BOLA.

This is relevant to organizations that need to understand their API inventory and identify weaknesses across cloud, on-premises, and hybrid environments. It is particularly important for companies with large API estates, multiple development teams, and complex integrations.

SherGuard API Abuse Intelligence

SherGuard's API Abuse Intelligence focuses on suspicious API traffic and usage patterns. It is designed to help businesses review activity such as repeated requests, bursts, suspicious clients, endpoint misuse, and credential-related abuse indicators.

Its API-first integration model allows a business to send relevant activity to SherGuard and use the resulting risk information within its own application workflow.

This is not the same as a full API security testing and vulnerability management program. Businesses still need secure API development practices, strong authentication and authorization, schema validation, secret management, penetration testing, and appropriate gateway controls.

Which approach fits your API requirements?

If your main concern is discovering undocumented APIs, assessing endpoint vulnerabilities, identifying BOLA risks, and protecting complex API environments, Imperva's API Security offering deserves close evaluation.

If your main concern is identifying suspicious usage patterns across your application workflows and connecting API risk with signup, device, bot, and payment signals, SherGuard may be relevant.

Some organizations may benefit from using both types of controls. API vulnerability protection and API abuse intelligence can complement one another rather than compete directly.

8. Detailed Comparison: Fake Signups and Account Abuse

Fake registrations can affect nearly every type of online business. SaaS providers may lose money through repeated free trials. Marketplaces may face fake buyers and sellers. E-commerce businesses may experience promotional abuse. Mobile applications may attract automated registrations that distort engagement metrics.

The challenge is that a registration request can look valid even when the account is being created for abuse.

Signals that can help identify suspicious registrations

  • Disposable or temporary email addresses.
  • Unusual registration velocity.
  • Repeated signup patterns across multiple accounts.
  • Suspicious device or browser environments.
  • Automated interaction patterns.
  • Repeated attempts to claim promotions or free trials.
  • Unusual network or geographic patterns.
  • Links between suspicious registration activity and later API or payment abuse.

No single indicator proves that a registration is fraudulent. A legitimate user may use a privacy-focused email provider, share a network with other users, or register from an unfamiliar device. Good signup protection combines signals and applies actions proportionate to risk.

SherGuard's signup-focused capabilities

SherGuard's Email Risk Intelligence is designed to analyze email-related risk signals, while Device Risk Intelligence and Bot Detection Intelligence provide additional context about the environment and behavior associated with a registration.

This connected approach can be useful for businesses that want to assess signup quality and suspicious account creation as part of a broader trust workflow.

Imperva's account and bot protection capabilities

Imperva's Advanced Bot Protection and Account Takeover Protection address automated threats and account-related attacks. These capabilities are relevant when fake account activity, automated login attempts, and account compromise are important concerns.

Buyers should confirm the exact signup-related detections available in the product configuration they are considering. Account takeover protection and fake signup detection are related but distinct requirements.

9. Detailed Comparison: Device Risk and Customer Trust

Device context can help businesses recognize patterns that are difficult to detect through account information alone. A user may create multiple accounts with different email addresses, but repeated activity from similar environments may indicate a connection between those accounts.

Device signals can also help identify unusual login environments, suspicious automation, and activity that deserves additional verification.

Why device intelligence matters

Passwords identify what a user knows. Email addresses identify a communication destination. Neither necessarily establishes whether the current session is trustworthy.

Device and environment information can provide another layer of context. For example, a new device attempting to access an account from an unusual environment may deserve more scrutiny when combined with unusual login behavior.

SherGuard's dedicated device module

SherGuard offers Device Risk Intelligence as one of its five core modules. It is designed to evaluate browser, operating system, screen, timezone, user-agent, automation, and other environment-related signals supplied through the integration.

This dedicated module is relevant to businesses that want device risk to be a visible part of their signup, login, bot, and payment review processes.

Evaluating device capabilities in Imperva

Device and client characteristics can contribute to bot and account protection decisions in security products. However, buyers should verify which device intelligence features, signals, history, and integrations are included in the particular Imperva products under consideration.

A dedicated device risk workflow and a device signal used within a bot mitigation product are not automatically equivalent. The distinction matters when a business needs persistent device analysis across registration, authentication, account activity, and checkout.

10. Detailed Comparison: Payment Fraud Prevention

Payment fraud creates a direct connection between digital abuse and financial loss. Attackers may use stolen payment credentials, automated card testing, compromised accounts, fake customer identities, or suspicious checkout behavior.

A business may have a secure payment processor and still need additional controls to identify risky activity before or during checkout.

Common payment fraud indicators

  • Repeated failed payment attempts.
  • Unusual transaction velocity.
  • Multiple cards used against one account or device.
  • Unusual billing and account information combinations.
  • Suspicious proxy or VPN indicators when relevant to the business's risk policy.
  • Automated checkout behavior.
  • Repeated low-value attempts consistent with card testing.
  • Unusual changes in account or payment behavior.

These signals should be interpreted carefully. A VPN can be used by a legitimate customer. A billing mismatch can have an innocent explanation. A failed transaction can result from a bank decline or an input error. Strong payment risk processes combine multiple indicators and avoid treating a single signal as conclusive evidence.

SherGuard Payment Fraud Intelligence

SherGuard includes a dedicated Payment Fraud Intelligence module intended to analyze suspicious payment activity and risk indicators. It can provide an additional risk assessment layer for businesses that want to review payment behavior alongside device, bot, and API signals.

Businesses should test the module against their actual checkout data, payment workflows, and fraud scenarios. They should also confirm which payment fields and contextual signals are available in their integration.

Imperva and payment-related abuse

Imperva's application and bot security capabilities can be relevant to automated activity targeting online checkout and other transactional workflows. Bot mitigation may help reduce automated abuse that affects payment pages or transaction endpoints.

However, bot mitigation should not automatically be treated as a complete payment fraud decisioning system. Businesses evaluating Imperva for transaction-level fraud prevention should confirm whether the specific payment risk analysis, payment integrations, and decision workflows they require are available.

Payment fraud prevention normally involves several controls working together: payment processor risk tools, transaction monitoring, authentication, device and behavioral signals, velocity controls, fraud rules, and human review for selected cases.

14. Limitations and Important Security Considerations

No single security platform can guarantee that a business will never experience a cyberattack, account compromise, fraudulent transaction, or service disruption. Security is a layered process that combines technology, architecture, policies, people, and ongoing monitoring.

What Imperva should not be assumed to replace

Even with a broad application security portfolio, organizations still need secure software development, identity and access management, strong authentication, vulnerability remediation, endpoint security, backup and recovery, incident response, and appropriate internal controls.

Buyers should also distinguish between Imperva's individual products. A feature described in one product's documentation should not automatically be assumed to be included in another product or every commercial package.

What SherGuard should not be assumed to replace

SherGuard's trust intelligence modules are designed to analyze risk signals across signup, device, bot, API, and payment workflows. They should not be treated as a complete replacement for a web application firewall, DDoS mitigation, secure coding, API vulnerability testing, endpoint protection, identity governance, or a dedicated security operations program.

Its risk outputs should be evaluated against real business data and integrated with appropriate application controls. A risk score or recommendation is useful only when the business understands the signals behind it and applies proportionate decisions.

False positives and legitimate users

Both automated detection and risk scoring can produce false positives. A legitimate customer may use a shared network, a new device, a privacy tool, or an unusual browser configuration. A business that blocks every unusual signal may create unnecessary friction and lose valid customers.

A safer deployment approach is to begin with monitoring, measure the results, tune the policies, and introduce enforcement gradually. High-risk activity may justify blocking, while uncertain activity may be better handled through additional verification or manual review.

Security requires more than detection

Detection identifies suspicious activity. Prevention requires an effective response. Businesses should define what happens when an event is flagged, who owns the decision, how exceptions are handled, and how the results are monitored.

A mature security process should also include incident response plans, audit logging, access controls, regular testing, secure secrets management, and a clear process for investigating suspicious activity.

A practical evaluation should begin with the organization's most expensive and frequent abuse problems. Avoid selecting a platform simply because it has the longest feature list. Identify the risks that affect revenue, customer trust, infrastructure, and operational workload.

Step 2: Identify the main threats

Determine whether your most important concerns are application vulnerabilities, API discovery, automated traffic, credential stuffing, fake registrations, device abuse, scraping, or payment fraud. Rank these threats according to their frequency and business impact.

Step 3: Match requirements to actual product capabilities

Compare the specific Imperva products and SherGuard modules relevant to each requirement. Do not assume that two features with similar names provide identical coverage. Ask vendors to demonstrate the exact detection, analysis, and response workflow.

Step 4: Test with representative traffic

Use a controlled test environment or a carefully managed monitoring period. Include legitimate user activity, known automation, suspicious registration patterns, API abuse simulations, and relevant payment risk scenarios.

Measure detection quality, false positives, latency, integration effort, operational visibility, and the time required to investigate an alert.

Step 5: Test enforcement carefully

Start with logging or monitoring where possible. Once the team understands the behavior of the system, introduce challenges, rate limits, verification steps, or blocking for clearly defined high-risk conditions.

Step 6: Review ongoing operational requirements

Confirm who will manage policies, review alerts, maintain integrations, investigate incidents, and respond to changes in attack patterns. Security tools need ongoing ownership to remain effective.
