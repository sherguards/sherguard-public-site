SherGuard vs HUMAN Security: Enterprise Bot Protection and Fraud Prevention Compared

A practical comparison of two different approaches to digital trust and application protection. Explore bot mitigation, account security, fake signups, device intelligence, API abuse, transaction fraud, AI agents, deployment considerations, and how to choose the right platform for your business.

Introduction

Modern fraud requires more than identifying automated traffic

Online businesses have become increasingly dependent on digital accounts, web applications, APIs, mobile experiences, and automated transactions. These systems make it easier to serve customers and expand into new markets, but they also create opportunities for attackers to exploit business processes.

A fraudulent campaign may begin with automated account creation. It may then move into credential abuse, suspicious account activity, API exploitation, promotional abuse, or fraudulent transactions. In some cases, attackers combine automated tools with human operators to make their activity harder to distinguish from legitimate customers.

This is why businesses need to think beyond a single security question such as whether a visitor is a bot. They also need to understand whether an account, device, request, or transaction presents an unacceptable business risk.

Two platforms that approach parts of this challenge are SherGuard and HUMAN Security.

HUMAN Security provides a broad application-protection portfolio that includes bot detection and mitigation, account protection, transaction-abuse controls, and capabilities for AI-agent visibility and governance. Its product documentation describes components such as the Sensor, Enforcer, and cloud-based Detector.

SherGuard is a Trust Intelligence Platform built around five core detection areas: email risk and fake signup detection, device risk intelligence, bot detection, API abuse detection, and payment fraud detection.

These platforms should not be considered identical products. Their product scope, architecture, and emphasis differ. The purpose of this comparison is to help businesses understand those differences and identify which capabilities deserve attention during a security-platform evaluation.

Security Fundamentals

Why bot detection and fraud prevention are not the same thing

One of the most important distinctions in application security is the difference between identifying automated traffic and evaluating whether a business interaction is trustworthy.

Bot detection focuses on identifying automated activity. Fraud prevention focuses on identifying activity that may produce financial loss, account compromise, abuse of business rules, or other harmful outcomes.

The two areas overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A bot may be harmless, while a human-operated account may be involved in fraud. A legitimate browser may be controlled by an attacker. An automated request may be technically valid but still violate a business rule.

Automated Traffic

Examples include scraping, automated login attempts, bulk registration, inventory hoarding, and repeated API requests.

Account Abuse

An account may be created for promotional abuse, spam, unauthorized access, or other activities that violate the business's rules.

Device Risk

A device may be associated with suspicious activity, repeated account creation, unusual access patterns, or other risk indicators.

API Abuse

An attacker may use valid endpoints in unintended ways, exploit weak authorization, automate expensive operations, or consume excessive resources.

Payment Fraud

Fraudulent transactions may involve stolen payment credentials, compromised accounts, card testing, or abusive purchasing patterns.

Human-Assisted Abuse

Some campaigns combine scripts, real people, compromised accounts, and automated services. This can make simplistic bot-versus-human classification insufficient.

OWASP's guidance on bot management makes a related point: businesses should not attempt to block every automated visitor. Search engines, monitoring systems, and other legitimate automated services may need access. Controls should be selected according to the endpoint, threat, and business impact. Real-World Scenarios

How these security capabilities apply to actual business problems

A useful platform comparison should consider the threats a business experiences, not just the names of individual product features.

Scenario 1: Fake Signup Campaign

A SaaS business offers a free trial. An attacker creates hundreds of accounts to repeatedly claim trial benefits, consume resources, or abuse promotional offers.

The business needs to examine registration patterns, email risk, device signals, automation, and the behavior of newly created accounts.

HUMAN documents fake-account protection through Account Defender. SherGuard addresses fake signup risk through Email Risk Intelligence, with device and bot-risk modules providing additional areas to evaluate.

Scenario 2: Credential Abuse

Attackers use previously exposed credentials to attempt access to customer accounts. Successful access may be followed by profile changes, unauthorized purchases, or theft of account value.

A business should evaluate authentication controls, rate limits, MFA, suspicious-session signals, and account-remediation workflows.

HUMAN documents Account Defender capabilities for compromised accounts. SherGuard's product should be evaluated according to the specific detection and response requirements of the business rather than assumed to provide identical account-remediation functionality.

Scenario 3: Automated API Abuse

A public API is used to perform repeated operations. The requests may use valid credentials but generate abnormal traffic, consume resources, or abuse business functionality.

Businesses should review authentication, authorization, request quotas, endpoint-level rate limits, logging, and suspicious usage patterns.

SherGuard includes API Abuse Intelligence as a core module. API security controls should still be layered with secure application design and infrastructure protections.

Scenario 4: Payment Abuse

An online store experiences repeated card testing, suspicious checkout activity, or automated attempts to purchase limited inventory.

The business needs to examine transaction patterns, account history, device signals, checkout behavior, and payment-provider controls.

HUMAN documents transaction-abuse protection for carding, scalping, and inventory hoarding. SherGuard includes Payment Fraud Intelligence as a core module.

Scenario 5: Suspicious Device Activity

A service sees multiple accounts interacting from devices or environments associated with suspicious behavior. The activity may involve automation, promotional abuse, or coordinated account activity.

Device-related signals can help security teams add context to email, account, bot, and transaction activity.

Device Risk Intelligence is one of SherGuard's core modules. HUMAN also documents device forensics as part of its application-protection approach.

Scenario 6: AI-Agent Traffic

An organization wants to understand whether visitors are people, automated crawlers, or AI agents. It may want to allow useful agents while restricting scraping or unwanted actions.

HUMAN documents AI-agent visibility and governance capabilities, including policies for trusted and unwanted activity.

Businesses should assess how agent identity is established, what actions can be governed, and how policies interact with existing access controls.

Architecture and Deployment

What should buyers examine before deploying a platform?

Detection quality is only one part of a security purchase. A platform must also fit the application's architecture, operational processes, privacy requirements, and response strategy.

Integration Requirements

Identify which web pages, APIs, authentication flows, mobile applications, and transaction paths need protection. Ask vendors to explain exactly where their sensors, agents, SDKs, or enforcement components operate.

Detection and Enforcement

Determine whether the product only identifies risk or can also take action. Possible actions include monitoring, alerting, challenging, rate limiting, blocking, or invoking an internal workflow.

False Positives

A legitimate customer should not be blocked simply because their device, location, or browsing pattern is unusual. Test controls against real customer journeys and measure the impact of challenges and blocks.

Operational Visibility

Security teams need understandable event details, useful reporting, investigation context, and the ability to identify why an interaction was treated as suspicious.

Privacy and Data Handling

Review the data collected, processing locations, retention periods, access controls, contractual commitments, and privacy implications of device and behavioral signals.

Performance and Reliability

Evaluate latency, availability, failure behavior, traffic handling, and the effect of security controls on high-volume customer journeys.

Pricing and Total Cost

Compare the complete cost of protection, not just the starting price

Important Considerations

What neither platform should be expected to replace

No bot-management or trust-intelligence platform should be treated as a complete substitute for secure software engineering and a layered security architecture.

Businesses should maintain appropriate controls for vulnerabilities, identity and access management, infrastructure security, data protection, backups, incident response, and secure payment processing.

Secure Application Development

Input validation, authorization checks, secure session handling, and protection against common application vulnerabilities remain essential.

Infrastructure Protection

Network security, DDoS mitigation, web application firewalls, and infrastructure monitoring may require separate controls.

Identity Security

Strong authentication, MFA, credential protection, and access-management policies remain necessary.

Payment Provider Controls

Payment processors, authorization decisions, chargeback procedures, and compliance controls remain part of payment security.

Incident Response

Organizations need documented procedures for investigating incidents, containing threats, recovering systems, and communicating with affected parties.

Continuous Testing

Security controls should be tested against changing attack patterns and the organization's real customer journeys.

Buyer Evaluation

A practical checklist for choosing between security platforms

Before selecting a platform, create a written list of the threats that matter most to your business. Use demonstrations and controlled tests to verify whether the proposed product addresses those requirements.

Security and Business Requirements

  • Which application endpoints are most frequently abused?
  • Does the business experience fake account creation?
  • Is credential stuffing or account takeover a major concern?
  • Does the business need device-risk intelligence?
  • Are APIs being abused or consumed at abnormal rates?
  • Does the business experience carding or payment fraud?
  • Does the organization need AI-agent visibility or governance?
  • Does it require client-side security capabilities?

Technical and Operational Requirements

  • Which integration methods are supported for the required products?
  • How are suspicious events explained and investigated?
  • Can the organization monitor before enabling blocking?
  • Can response actions be customized?
  • How are legitimate users protected from false positives?
  • What is the impact on application latency and availability?
  • What data is collected and how long is it retained?
  • What happens if a detection or enforcement component is unavailable?

Commercial Requirements

  • What is the total annual cost for the required modules?
  • How is usage measured and billed?
  • Are there minimum commitments or overage charges?
  • What implementation and support resources are included?
  • Can the organization test the product against realistic traffic?
