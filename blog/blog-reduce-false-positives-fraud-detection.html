Fraud Detection and Risk Management

How to Reduce False Positives in Fraud Detection Without Increasing Fraud Risk

Fraud detection systems must identify suspicious activity without unnecessarily blocking legitimate customers. Learn why false positives happen, how they affect businesses, and how to improve fraud decisions through better data, risk-based controls, monitoring, and continuous calibration.

Introduction

Fraud prevention must protect legitimate customers too

Modern online businesses face a difficult security challenge. They must identify fraudulent activity quickly while allowing genuine customers to register, access accounts, use applications, and complete payments without unnecessary interruptions.

Fraud detection systems are designed to identify unusual activity and potential threats. However, suspicious-looking activity is not always fraudulent. A legitimate customer might travel, replace a device, change payment methods, make an unusually large purchase, or access an account from a new location.

If a fraud detection system interprets every unusual event as proof of fraud, legitimate users may be blocked or subjected to unnecessary verification.

This is known as a false positive. In payment environments, a false positive may result in a legitimate transaction being declined because the system incorrectly identifies it as suspicious.

False positives can create lost revenue, abandoned purchases, customer complaints, additional support work, and damage to customer trust. They can also increase the workload of fraud investigation teams.

The objective should not be to eliminate every security challenge or approve every transaction. Businesses need a balanced approach that distinguishes between genuine customers, suspicious activity, and confirmed fraud.

This guide explains how organizations can reduce false positives without weakening their fraud controls.

Understanding the Problem

What are false positives in fraud detection?

A false positive occurs when a detection system incorrectly identifies legitimate activity as suspicious or fraudulent.

For example, an online store might receive an order from an established customer who is travelling internationally. The customer uses a new device and makes a purchase that is larger than their usual order.

A fraud system may interpret the unfamiliar device, location, and transaction amount as multiple warning signs. However, the customer may be acting legitimately.

If the system automatically declines the payment without considering additional context, the business has generated a false positive.

Legitimate Payment Declined

A genuine customer attempts to complete a purchase, but the payment is declined because a risk rule incorrectly identifies the transaction as fraudulent.

Valid Account Restricted

A customer signs in from a new device or location and is incorrectly treated as an account security threat.

Genuine Signup Flagged

A legitimate user creates an account using an email provider, network, or device characteristic that triggers a suspicious registration rule.

Normal API Activity Flagged

A legitimate application generates a temporary increase in API requests that resembles automated abuse or unusual traffic.

False Positives vs False Negatives

Why reducing false positives must not increase fraud losses

Fraud detection systems must consider two different types of mistakes. Understanding the distinction is essential when evaluating detection performance.

False Positive

Legitimate activity is incorrectly identified as suspicious or fraudulent.

The business may reject a genuine payment, restrict a valid account, or request unnecessary verification.

The result can be customer friction, lost revenue, and additional operational costs.

False Negative

Fraudulent activity is incorrectly classified as legitimate.

A suspicious payment, fake account, abusive API request, or automated attack may pass through the detection process.

The result can be financial losses, account abuse, chargebacks, operational disruption, or other security problems.

The four possible detection outcomes

A useful way to evaluate a fraud detection system is to compare its decisions against the actual outcomes.

  • True positive: Fraudulent activity is correctly identified.
  • True negative: Legitimate activity is correctly identified as legitimate.
  • False positive: Legitimate activity is incorrectly flagged.
  • False negative: Fraudulent activity is incorrectly allowed through.

A system that blocks almost everything may appear strict, but it is not necessarily effective. A system that approves almost everything may provide a smooth customer experience while exposing the business to unacceptable fraud risk.

Business Impact

How false positives affect revenue and customer experience

False positives are not simply technical classification errors. They can affect the financial performance and reputation of an organization.

Their impact may extend beyond the original transaction because customers who experience repeated security problems may decide to use another service.

Lost Revenue

Legitimate purchases may be declined, preventing businesses from completing transactions that would otherwise have generated revenue.

Customer Abandonment

Customers who encounter repeated payment failures or unnecessary verification may abandon checkout or stop using the service.

Higher Support Costs

Support teams may need to investigate declined payments, restricted accounts, registration problems, and customer complaints.

Investigation Workload

Fraud teams may spend significant time reviewing alerts that do not represent actual fraud, reducing the time available for genuine threats.

Reduced Customer Trust

Legitimate users may lose confidence in a business when ordinary account activity or purchases repeatedly trigger security restrictions.

Operational Inefficiency

Poorly calibrated rules can create unnecessary manual reviews, inconsistent decisions, and additional pressure on operational teams.

Common Causes

Why do fraud detection systems generate false positives?

False positives can originate from data problems, overly broad rules, incomplete customer context, outdated assumptions, or detection models that are not properly calibrated.

Businesses should investigate the reason behind incorrect decisions instead of simply reducing every risk threshold.

1. Overly Aggressive Rules

A business may create a rule that blocks every transaction above a particular amount or every login from an unfamiliar location.

Such rules may catch some suspicious activity, but they can also affect legitimate customers whose behavior is unusual for understandable reasons.

2. Incomplete Customer Information

A system may evaluate a transaction without sufficient information about the customer's previous activity, account history, or normal purchasing behavior.

Missing context can make an ordinary event appear more suspicious than it actually is.

3. Unreliable or Outdated Data

Incorrect device information, stale reputation data, incomplete records, or inconsistent transaction fields can influence risk decisions.

Data quality should be reviewed as part of fraud detection operations.

4. Treating One Signal as Proof

A new device, unusual IP address, disposable-looking email pattern, or high transaction amount does not automatically prove fraud.

Individual indicators should be evaluated in the context of the activity being investigated.

5. Changing Customer Behavior

Customers may travel, replace devices, change internet providers, update payment methods, or make purchases that differ from their previous behavior.

Rules that assume customer behavior never changes can produce unnecessary alerts.

6. Poorly Maintained Detection Models

Fraud patterns and legitimate customer behavior evolve. A detection model that is not periodically evaluated may become less appropriate for current activity.

Continuous testing and calibration are important parts of maintaining detection quality.

Strategy 1

Use risk-based decisions instead of treating every alert equally

One of the most important ways to reduce false positives is to avoid treating every suspicious indicator as a reason for immediate rejection.

A risk-based approach evaluates the seriousness of the activity and determines an appropriate response. Different levels of risk can justify different actions.

For example, an unfamiliar device may justify additional verification, while a confirmed malicious pattern may justify blocking the activity.

Strategy 2

Evaluate multiple signals instead of relying on one indicator

A single signal rarely provides enough information to make every fraud decision confidently.

Consider a customer who logs in from a new device. That event may be unusual, but it is not necessarily malicious. The business should consider additional context before deciding whether to allow, challenge, monitor, or restrict the activity.

Depending on the workflow, useful information may include account history, device characteristics, email risk, request behavior, transaction details, and previous activity.

Email and Signup Signals

Examine registration patterns, suspicious email characteristics, repeated account creation, and other indicators associated with signup abuse.

An unfamiliar email address should not automatically be treated as proof that a real person is fraudulent.

Device Risk Signals

Evaluate device and browser characteristics alongside other available information.

Device changes and unusual environments can occur during legitimate customer activity.

Behavior and Bot Signals

Review repeated actions, unusual request timing, automated interaction patterns, and excessive activity.

High activity alone does not always mean that a legitimate application or user is malicious.

API Usage Signals

Evaluate request frequency, endpoint usage, repeated calls, and suspicious access patterns.

Expected traffic from a legitimate integration should be distinguished from abusive automation.

Payment Risk Signals

Consider transaction amount, payment history, repeated attempts, billing information, and other available risk indicators.

A larger-than-usual purchase may require context, not an automatic decline.

Historical Activity

Where available and appropriate, consider previous successful activity, account age, transaction history, and known behavior.

Historical information can provide useful context, although it should not override evidence of current fraud.

Strategy 3

Build a proper risk scoring and threshold strategy

Fraud scoring can help organizations evaluate activity consistently. Rather than making every decision through a separate isolated rule, a risk score can represent the combined assessment of relevant indicators.

However, a score is only useful when the business understands what it represents, how it was produced, and how its thresholds affect actual outcomes.

Define the Risk Categories

Establish clear categories such as low, medium, and high risk. Define what each category means for the specific workflow being protected.

Choose Appropriate Actions

Decide whether each category should result in allowing, monitoring, verification, manual review, or blocking.

Test the Thresholds

Evaluate how different thresholds affect legitimate activity and confirmed fraud using representative historical or controlled test data.

Review Business Impact

Measure the effect of threshold changes on customer experience, fraud losses, manual reviews, and operational costs.

Illustrative risk decision table

The following example shows how different risk categories might be handled. These are conceptual categories, not SherGuard's published scoring thresholds or recommended production settings.

  • Low risk: Allow the activity and retain appropriate monitoring.
  • Medium risk: Request additional verification or send the activity for review.
  • High risk: Apply stronger controls, escalate, or block when supported by sufficient evidence.

Thresholds should be established using actual business data and regularly reviewed. A threshold that works for one company may not be suitable for another.

Strategy 4

Use additional verification when the evidence is uncertain

Blocking is not the only possible response to suspicious activity.

When the available evidence is incomplete, additional verification can help a business distinguish legitimate users from potential attackers.

The verification method should be proportionate to the risk and should avoid introducing unnecessary friction for ordinary customer activity.

Step-Up Authentication

Request additional authentication when a login or account action presents meaningful risk indicators.

Payment Verification

Use appropriate payment authentication or verification processes when a transaction requires additional confidence.

Manual Review

Send selected uncertain cases to trained reviewers rather than automatically rejecting every unusual event.

Temporary Monitoring

Monitor selected activity more closely when the available information does not justify an immediate block.

Strategy 5

Measure false positives with the right performance metrics

Organizations cannot reliably improve fraud detection quality if they only measure the number of blocked transactions or detected alerts.

A useful measurement framework should evaluate both security effectiveness and the impact on legitimate activity.

False Positive Rate

Measures how often legitimate activity is incorrectly classified as positive or suspicious within the defined evaluation population.

Precision

Measures the proportion of flagged positive cases that are actually positive according to the available ground truth.

Recall

Measures the proportion of actual positive cases that the system successfully identifies.

False Negative Rate

Measures how often actual fraud or other target events are incorrectly classified as negative.

False Decline Rate

Tracks legitimate payment attempts incorrectly declined by the relevant decision process.

Manual Review Rate

Measures how much activity is escalated for human investigation and how that workload changes over time.

Understand precision and recall

Precision and recall help explain the trade-offs in fraud detection.

Precision: Of the cases identified as positive by the system, how many were actually positive?

Recall: Of all the actual positive cases, how many did the system successfully identify?

A business should evaluate these metrics together. Improving one measure without examining the other can create unintended consequences.

The definition of a positive case must also be clear. For example, a suspicious event, a confirmed fraudulent payment, and a customer dispute are not necessarily interchangeable labels.

Strategy 6

Create a feedback loop for incorrect fraud decisions

Fraud detection systems should not be treated as permanent configurations that never require review.

Businesses need a process for examining incorrect decisions, understanding why they occurred, and deciding whether the underlying rules, data, or models need adjustment.

Collect Review Outcomes

Record whether investigated alerts were confirmed fraud, legitimate activity, or unresolved cases.

Identify Repeated Patterns

Look for recurring false positives associated with particular rules, devices, transaction types, or customer workflows.

Investigate Root Causes

Determine whether the issue originates from inaccurate data, an overly broad rule, missing context, or an unsuitable threshold.

Test Proposed Changes

Evaluate changes against representative historical data or controlled scenarios before applying them broadly.

Document Decisions

Maintain records of configuration changes, the reason for each change, and the expected impact.

Review Performance Again

Monitor subsequent outcomes to determine whether the adjustment improved detection quality without increasing fraud exposure.

Strategy 7

Improve data quality before changing detection rules

Poor data quality can cause a fraud detection system to make incorrect decisions even when its rules appear reasonable.

Before changing risk thresholds, organizations should examine the information supplied to the detection process.

Data Completeness

Identify missing fields and incomplete records that may affect risk evaluation.

Data Accuracy

Check whether device, account, transaction, and other relevant information is accurate.

Data Timeliness

Ensure that important information is available at the appropriate time for the decision being made.

Consistent Data Mapping

Make sure different systems interpret and transmit important fields consistently.

Duplicate and Conflicting Records

Review duplicate records and conflicting information that may distort the evaluation.

Integration Testing

Test data flows and integrations after application or configuration changes.

Practical Examples

How businesses can reduce false positives in real situations

The best response depends on the business workflow. The following examples illustrate how organizations can avoid unnecessary restrictions while maintaining appropriate security controls.

Example 1: E-Commerce Payment

A returning customer makes a purchase from a new device while travelling.

A basic rule might decline the payment because the location and device are unfamiliar.

A more contextual process could consider transaction history, payment information, device indicators, and appropriate authentication before deciding whether to approve or escalate the transaction.

Example 2: SaaS Registration

A legitimate business user registers using an email domain that has not previously appeared in the platform.

An overly restrictive rule may classify the registration as suspicious.

The business can evaluate email risk, registration behavior, device context, and other available signals before restricting the account.

Example 3: Mobile Application

A customer upgrades their phone and signs into an existing account.

A device-based rule may treat the unfamiliar environment as suspicious.

The application can use suitable authentication and additional context instead of assuming that every new device represents an attacker.

Example 4: API Integration

A legitimate partner application temporarily increases its API request volume during a scheduled operation.

A broad request-volume rule may identify the activity as abusive.

The business should compare the requests against expected integration behavior, endpoint usage, and other available indicators.

Example 5: Payment Verification

A customer attempts a transaction that triggers an unusual activity rule.

Instead of automatically declining every uncertain case, the business may apply an appropriate additional verification step.

The final decision should consider the result of that verification and the wider risk context.

Example 6: Promotional Activity

A business runs a campaign that generates a large number of legitimate registrations in a short period.

A registration velocity rule may incorrectly flag genuine campaign traffic.

The business should evaluate campaign context, account characteristics, and suspicious behavior rather than relying on volume alone.

Testing and Calibration

Test fraud detection changes before deploying them

Reducing false positives should be an evidence-based process. A change that appears beneficial in one situation may weaken protection in another.

Organizations should evaluate proposed changes using representative data and realistic business scenarios.

A practical testing process

  1. Define the problem. Identify the rule, model, or workflow responsible for the incorrect decisions.
  2. Establish a baseline. Record existing false positive outcomes, fraud outcomes, review volume, and relevant customer impact.
  3. Prepare representative test data. Include legitimate activity, known fraud examples, and uncertain cases where possible.
  4. Test the proposed adjustment. Compare the new decision logic with the existing configuration.
  5. Examine trade-offs. Check whether fewer false positives are accompanied by increased false negatives.
  6. Deploy carefully. Use an appropriate rollout and monitoring process.
  7. Review actual results. Confirm whether the change produces the intended improvement under real operating conditions.

Organizations should also retain records of rule changes, test results, approvals, and the reasons behind important configuration decisions.

Regulatory and Industry Guidance

Why ongoing calibration and feedback matter

Fraud detection optimization is also reflected in financial-sector guidance.

The Saudi Central Bank's Fraud Control Framework includes requirements and guidance concerning fraud detection systems, data quality, testing, calibration, and performance monitoring for organizations within its applicable scope.

Its fraud detection systems guidance specifically addresses testing detection scenarios, reviewing precision and recall, examining false positives and false negatives, and periodically reviewing rules and parameters.

This supports an important operational principle: fraud detection should be monitored and improved continuously rather than treated as a one-time configuration exercise.

Visa's published fraud prevention resources also discuss the importance of identifying genuine customers, reducing false declines, and balancing fraud controls with payment acceptance and customer experience.

These resources provide useful industry context. Organizations should always determine which regulatory requirements apply to their own jurisdiction, business model, and activities.

Frequently Asked Questions

False positives in fraud detection: common questions

What is a false positive in fraud detection?

A false positive occurs when legitimate activity is incorrectly identified as suspicious or fraudulent. For example, a genuine payment may be declined because a system incorrectly interprets the transaction as risky.

Why do fraud detection systems produce false positives?

Common causes include overly aggressive rules, incomplete data, changing customer behavior, insufficient context, unreliable signals, and detection models that have not been properly calibrated.

How can businesses reduce false positives?

Businesses can improve data quality, evaluate multiple signals, use risk-based decisions, test thresholds, introduce appropriate verification, and review incorrect detection outcomes.

Does reducing false positives increase fraud risk?

It can if controls are weakened without evaluating false negatives and fraud losses. Changes should be tested against both legitimate activity and confirmed fraud scenarios.

What is the difference between precision and recall?

Precision measures how many flagged positive cases are actually positive. Recall measures how many actual positive cases the system successfully identifies. Both help evaluate detection performance.

Should every suspicious transaction be declined?

Not necessarily. Depending on the evidence and risk level, a business may allow, monitor, request additional verification, escalate for review, or decline the activity.

How often should fraud detection rules be reviewed?

Rules should be reviewed periodically and when meaningful changes occur in threats, customer behavior, business operations, or data quality. Review frequency should reflect the organization's risk and operational requirements.

Can device information cause false positives?

Yes. Customers can change devices, browsers, networks, or locations for legitimate reasons. Device information should be interpreted alongside other relevant signals.

What should businesses measure besides fraud losses?

Useful measures include false positive rates, false decline rates, precision, recall, manual review rates, customer friction, and operational costs.

Can AI eliminate false positives?

No detection approach should be assumed to eliminate all false positives. AI-based systems can help evaluate complex patterns, but their performance depends on data quality, validation, monitoring, and appropriate decision policies.

Conclusion

Better fraud detection means protecting customers and the business

False positives are an important challenge for organizations that depend on digital accounts, online payments, APIs, and automated customer workflows.

Blocking suspicious activity is necessary, but incorrectly blocking legitimate users can create lost revenue, unnecessary operational work, and damage to customer relationships.

The most effective approach is not simply to make fraud rules more restrictive or more permissive. Businesses should improve data quality, evaluate multiple signals, use risk-based responses, measure detection performance, and continuously review incorrect decisions.

Precision, recall, false positive rates, false negative rates, customer experience, and fraud losses should be considered together.

Fraud detection is an ongoing process. Regular testing, calibration, feedback, and monitoring help organizations maintain a better balance between security and legitimate customer activity.

A strong fraud strategy should protect the business without treating every unusual customer action as proof of wrongdoing.
